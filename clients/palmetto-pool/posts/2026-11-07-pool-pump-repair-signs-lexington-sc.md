Your pool pump is the heart of your pool.

It keeps water moving, pushes it through the filter, distributes your chemicals, and prevents the stagnation that turns a backyard oasis into a swamp. When it's working, you barely think about it. When it isn't — you'll know fast.

For pool owners in Lexington, SC, the problem is that pump issues don't always announce themselves loudly or all at once. They tend to show up gradually, as small quirks that are easy to dismiss until they become big, expensive problems.

Here are five signs your [pool pump](/services/pool-pump-filter-repair/) is telling you something's wrong — and what to do about each one.

## 1. Your Pool Pump Is Making Unusual Noises

A healthy pump runs with a steady, low hum. If yours has started making grinding, screeching, rattling, or gurgling sounds, something has changed — and it's not good.

**What different noises usually mean:**

- **Grinding or screeching** — worn bearings. This is one of the most common failure points as pumps age. The motor bearings wear down over time, especially in a hot, humid climate like Lexington's. Left alone, the motor will eventually seize completely.
- **Rattling** — loose debris in the impeller housing or a loose component. Sometimes it's as simple as a leaf or small stone that made its way past the skimmer basket. Other times it signals internal damage.
- **Gurgling or air-sucking sounds** — air is getting into the system somewhere it shouldn't be. This is usually a suction-side leak — a loose fitting, cracked lid O-ring, or damaged valve. Air in the pump creates cavitation, which accelerates wear and reduces circulation efficiency.

**What to do:** Don't run a pump that's grinding or screeching. That motor is in trouble. [Call a pool tech](/contact/) to diagnose whether it's a bearing replacement, impeller issue, or full motor/pump replacement.

## 2. Weak Water Flow or Poor Circulation

Walk up to your pool's return jets. Is the water coming through them with reasonable pressure? Or does it feel sluggish — barely moving?

Weak return flow almost always means something is restricting circulation. The most common culprits:

- **Clogged or dirty filter** — your pool filter (sand, cartridge, or DE) needs regular cleaning or backwashing. A heavily loaded filter creates so much resistance that the pump can't push water through it effectively.
- **Blocked skimmer or pump basket** — a jammed basket restricts the water volume getting to the pump.
- **Failing impeller** — the impeller is the spinning component inside the pump that moves the water. If it's cracked, clogged with debris, or starting to fail, flow drops significantly.
- **Undersized pump for the system** — if your pool was recently replumbed or equipment was changed, the pump may no longer be matched correctly to your setup.

Poor circulation also means your sanitizers aren't being distributed properly throughout the pool — which leads to water chemistry problems and algae growth, even when your chemical levels test fine on paper.

## 3. Your Pump Won't Prime (or Loses Prime Frequently)

Priming is the process by which your pump draws water from the pool to get circulation started. A pump that won't prime — or one that primes but then loses the water column and runs dry — has a problem that needs attention.

Causes include:

- Low water level in the pool (easy fix — add water)
- Air leaks on the suction side of the plumbing
- A damaged or improperly seated pump lid or lid O-ring
- A cracked or degraded pump housing

Running a pump dry — even briefly — generates significant heat and can damage the seal, the impeller, and even the motor. If your pump is losing prime regularly, don't just restart it and hope for the best. Get it checked.

## 4. The Pump Is Tripping Your Circuit Breaker

If your pump repeatedly trips its breaker, there's an electrical issue that needs to be diagnosed by a professional — full stop.

It could be:

- A failing capacitor (the component that helps the motor start)
- A shorted or overheating motor
- Incorrect voltage reaching the pump
- A ground fault in the wiring

Repeatedly resetting the breaker and running the pump anyway is dangerous. Pool pumps operate near water, and electrical faults in that environment are serious. Have a professional diagnose it before you run it again.

## 5. The Pump Is Old — and You Keep Repairing It

Most pool pumps have a lifespan of 8–15 years, depending on usage, maintenance, and equipment quality. If yours is approaching or past that range and you're calling for repairs every season, it's time for the math conversation.

The rule of thumb: if a repair costs more than 50% of the cost of a new pump, replacement is usually the smarter investment. A new [variable-speed pump](/services/pool-equipment-upgrades/) will also run significantly more efficiently than an older single-speed model — which typically translates to real savings on your monthly electric bill.
