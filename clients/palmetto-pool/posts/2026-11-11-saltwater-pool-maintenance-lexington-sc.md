A lot of Lexington homeowners switch to saltwater because they've heard it's "maintenance-free." Salt pools *are* easier in a lot of ways: softer-feeling water, no jugs of chlorine to haul, steadier sanitizer levels. But the part that makes the chlorine (the salt cell) needs regular care. Skip it, and you'll find out the expensive way.

Here's what a salt system actually needs through a typical Midlands year.

## How a salt pool works, in one paragraph

Your pool isn't sanitized by salt. It's sanitized by chlorine, just like any other pool. The salt cell uses electricity to turn dissolved salt into chlorine as water flows through it. That's why the cell matters so much: if it's coated in scale or worn out, your pool quietly stops making enough chlorine. Then cloudy water and algae show up, usually on the hottest week of the year.

## The three things a salt cell needs

### 1. Regular cleaning to remove scale

Calcium scale builds up on the cell's plates over time, especially in hot weather and when pH runs high. Scale blocks the plates from doing their job and makes the cell work harder.

- **How often:** we clean cells about **twice a year**, plus any time an inspection shows buildup.
- **Signs it's time:** white, crusty deposits on the plates, a "check cell" or low-output light, or chlorine readings that keep slipping even though the system is set high.
- **A caution:** over-cleaning with acid shortens a cell's life. Clean when it's needed, following the manufacturer's directions, not every time you think of it.

### 2. The right salt level

Every system has a target salt range listed on the unit or in the manual. Most residential systems sit somewhere around 2,700–3,400 ppm, but **check your specific model**. Too low and the cell can't produce; too high and you risk damage and error codes. Heavy summer storms can dilute your salt level, so it's worth testing after big rain.

### 3. Protection in cold water

Most salt cells stop producing chlorine once water temperatures drop low enough, often somewhere in the 50s or low 60s depending on the model. Running a cell that can't produce just adds wear. That's why many Midlands pool owners **swap in a "dummy" cell for winter** and store the real one, then reinstall it in spring.

## A Midlands salt pool calendar

- **Spring:** reinstall your cell if it was stored, check salt level, and clean the cell before pollen season and the summer heat hit.
- **Summer:** test weekly. Heat raises chlorine demand, and storms can drop your salt level. Watch pH, since high pH speeds up scale.
- **Fall:** a second cleaning is a good idea before the cell slows down for winter.
- **Winter:** swap to a dummy cell, keep the pump running on freezing nights, and keep an eye on chemistry every couple of weeks.

## How long does a salt cell last?

It depends on the cell, run time, and how it's cared for. Many last somewhere around 3–7 years. Water chemistry and scale are the two biggest factors you can control. Balanced pH and on-schedule cleaning are the cheapest insurance you can buy for a part that's expensive to replace.

## Signs your salt cell may be failing

- Chlorine stays low even with the system turned up
- The cell needs cleaning far more often than it used to
- Persistent error lights after cleaning and correcting salt level
- Plates look worn or damaged
