If you've owned a pool in the Lexington area for more than one summer, you already know: August is different.

May is manageable. June is busy. July starts pushing your luck. But August? August is when pool chemistry fights back. Water that was perfectly balanced two weeks ago starts going haywire. Chlorine disappears overnight. Algae appears out of nowhere on a Tuesday. Homeowners who coasted through June suddenly find themselves staring at green water.

It's not bad luck. It's science — and once you understand what's happening, it makes a lot more sense.

Here's what extreme summer heat actually does to your pool, and what it takes to stay ahead of it in the South Carolina Midlands.

## Why August Is the Hardest Month for Your Pool

### The UV Problem

South Carolina sits at a latitude where August UV index readings regularly hit 10 or 11 — classified as "Very High" to "Extreme." That UV radiation doesn't just tan your skin. It aggressively breaks down free chlorine in your water.

An [unprotected outdoor pool](/services/weekly-pool-maintenance/) can lose **50–90% of its chlorine within 2 hours** of direct sunlight. That's not an exaggeration — it's one of the most common reasons pool chemistry fails in summer, and it catches homeowners completely off guard.

If your pool uses stabilized chlorine (which contains cyanuric acid), you're partially protected. But in the peak of a South Carolina August, even stabilized pools need more frequent attention than most homeowners expect.

### The Heat Itself

High water temperatures speed up every chemical reaction in your pool — including the ones you don't want. Warm water accelerates bacterial growth, promotes algae reproduction, and burns through sanitizer faster. The sweet spot for pool water chemistry is around 78°F. By August, most unshaded residential pools in the Lexington area are sitting closer to 85–90°F.

At those temperatures:

- Algae can bloom in as little as **24 hours** if chlorine dips
- Chlorine demand can double or triple compared to spring levels
- Phosphates — algae's food source — build up faster from bather load and debris

### The Bather Load Effect

August is also when pools get used the most. Kids are home from school. Neighbors come over. Birthday parties happen. Every person who gets into your pool brings in sweat, sunscreen, body oils, and organic matter that all consume chlorine.

Those compounds bind with chlorine and form **chloramines** — that's the "pool smell" people associate with over-chlorinated water. In reality, that smell means your chlorine is tied up and not sanitizing. The fix isn't less chlorine — it's a shock treatment to break those bonds and restore free chlorine to working levels.

## What an August Maintenance Schedule Actually Looks Like

Keeping a pool clean and safe in August requires more than what worked in April. Here's what's realistic for the Midlands climate:

**Testing frequency:** At minimum twice a week — ideally every other day during peak heat. A basic test should cover pH, free chlorine, and alkalinity. A full panel (including cyanuric acid, calcium hardness, and phosphates) should be run at least once or twice a month.

**Chlorine levels:** Target 2–4 ppm free chlorine in August, not the 1–2 ppm that's fine in cooler months. Your pool is burning through it faster, so you need to maintain a higher baseline.

**Shocking:** Plan on shocking the pool **at least once a week** in August, ideally on Friday evening before weekend use. Let the pump run overnight so it circulates fully before anyone gets in.

**Pump run time:** 10–12 hours per day minimum. Many professionals recommend running your pump during the hottest part of the day when chemical demand is highest.

**Brushing:** Brush the walls and steps twice a week. Algae attaches to surfaces before it becomes visible in the water. Brushing disrupts that attachment and keeps it in suspension where your sanitizer and filter can deal with it.

## The Warning Signs That August Is Winning

If any of these look familiar, your pool is already falling behind:

- **Water looks slightly hazy or dull** — early-stage algae or chemistry imbalance
- **Chlorine reads zero even after adding product** — high demand is consuming it faster than you're adding it
- **Green tint, especially in corners or on steps** — algae has established
- **Skin or eye irritation after swimming** — chloramines or pH out of range
- **Strong "chlorine" smell** — counterintuitively, means your chlorine isn't working
- **Filter pressure 8–10 psi above normal** — filter is clogged and not cleaning effectively

Any one of these caught early is a straightforward fix. Left alone for a week in August heat, each one can escalate fast.
